Verdict

CC Pathfinder is described as a one-stop solution for all document-related operational needs, serving both business consumers and operational engineers. That is a product statement. Architecturally it hides three systems with almost nothing in common.

The proposed design routes all three through one chat surface, one agent chain, one orchestrator, one identity. The SRE equivalent is putting the customer status page, the read-only ops console, and the production runbook executor in the same deployment behind the same service account. You would not accept that for a service. The word "agent" in front of it does not change the calculus.

The blast radius argument settles it on its own. A hallucination in the FAQ path costs a confused product owner. The same model, in the same process, one hop from an orchestrator that can act on eSign and CDS, costs a change record nobody can explain to audit.

Second structural problem: the Rule Agent holds capability knowledge for systems it does not own. The application teams own their runbooks. A central mirror of those runbooks goes stale the moment it is built. This is the enterprise service bus anti-pattern with a language model bolted on, and it is also the stale-central-runbook-repository problem most SRE organisations have already lost once.

The policy engine contract

One sentence: it owns the answer to "may this identity perform this action on this resource, right now" and nothing else. Everything it owns follows from keeping that answer deterministic, fast, and explainable after the fact.

What it owns

  • The authorization decision. Four inputs always: subject (the propagated end-user identity, never the agent's service account), action (a registered capability id), resource (type, id, attributes from a system of record), context (channel, session, incident, approval state, time).
  • Risk-tier registration. Every capability declares its tier on registration. Untiered means denied. This is what turns the tier boundary from a diagram convention into something a machine enforces.
  • Obligations, not just verdicts. A permit can carry requirements: redact these fields, require approval, dry-run first, expire in 90 seconds, log to this stream. Obligations buy granularity without a combinatorial rule explosion.
  • Data-scoping predicates. Not "may they call the status API" but "which envelopes may they see", returned as a filter applied at query time.
  • Approval and break-glass rules. Which actions need a human, who qualifies, what the emergency path costs in extra logging.
  • Deny by default. Unknown capability, unresolvable resource, unverifiable identity: deny. Fail closed, always.
  • The decision log and policy versioning. Every decision records the policy version that produced it, joined to the trace id. This is the artefact you hand to audit.

What it must not own

  • Business rules. "An envelope can only be voided while in Sent status" is eSign's domain logic and lives in eSign's capability server. Policy answers may you; the capability server answers is this valid. Let domain rules migrate in and you have rebuilt the Rule Agent monolith under a new name, deliberately this time.
  • Tool selection. The model proposes, policy disposes. The moment policy chooses what to invoke, a probabilistic decision is back in the deterministic seat.
  • Prompt guardrails. Injection detection, moderation, PII scrubbing. Different component, different failure mode, different tuning cadence. Do not fuse them because both sometimes say no.
  • Anything the model can influence. Inputs come from verified tokens, the capability registry, and systems of record. Never from retrieved text or model output. If a KB document can affect a policy input, you have built an injection path into authorization.

The four things that will break it

  • Latency. Policy sits on every tool call in an interactive path. Embedded or sidecar evaluation against a distributed bundle, sub-10ms at p99. A network hop to a central service puts a synchronous dependency in the hot path of everything, and you already know how that ends.
  • Availability. Fail-closed is correct, so the decision point's availability budget must exceed the sum of everything behind it. Local evaluation with a cached bundle solves this and latency together. A central service solves neither.
  • Identity propagation. If the end user's identity does not survive the hop from supervisor to tier to capability server, policy is evaluating the agent's permissions and you have built expensive theatre. Token exchange or on-behalf-of flows. Prove this end to end before writing a single rule.
  • Authoring bottleneck. If your team writes every rule you are the constraint. If application teams write freely, guardrails erode. Two layers: central invariants that always apply and cannot be loosened, plus team rules that can only narrow. Same shape as an admission controller with delegated namespace policy.

The test for whether the boundary is drawn right: an application team should be able to change their entire runbook without touching policy, and you should be able to tighten an entitlement rule across all twelve services without touching a single application team's code. If either requires a coordinated release, the boundary is in the wrong place.

Sequencing

Ship in risk order, not capability order. Each phase has an entry gate that is a demonstrated property, not a milestone date.

Phase 1 — knowledge tier alone

Chat plus retrieval over a curated knowledge base. Zero tool access, zero write paths, no orchestrator. This carries most of the FAQ and onboarding value described in the brief, can be live in weeks, and its worst failure is a poor answer to a low-stakes question.

It also earns the eval harness and feedback loop cheaply, before the stakes rise. Measure deflection rate and answer correctness against a golden set. Do not measure usage.

Exit gate: golden-set accuracy above an agreed threshold, and a working feedback loop with named curators per corpus

Phase 2 — state tier

Deterministic, entitlement-scoped reads into eSign, CDS, CX. The model selects the capability; the policy engine and the capability server enforce. Answers to state questions are grounded strictly in tool output, never in retrieved text.

Entry gate: a user's own entitlement demonstrably reaches the downstream call, proven end to end. Not a design intent. A working trace.

Phase 3 — operate tier, and possibly never as chat

Incident-triggered investigation as a durable workflow. Runbooks published by owning teams as capability servers. Approval before mutation, dry-run by default, idempotency keys, per-capability circuit breakers, a kill switch that a single on-call engineer can reach in under a minute.

Separate deployment. Separate credentials. Separate failure domain from the estate it diagnoses.

Entry gate: failure-domain separation demonstrated by a game day where the estate is degraded and Pathfinder still answers

Follow-up threads

Each of these needs a working session rather than an answer in a review document.

  • Intent taxonomy. Enumerate the real question set from existing support tickets and ccintake traffic. Classify each as retrieval, lookup, or investigation. Then count what fraction genuinely needs dynamic planning rather than a classified function call. If that fraction is under twenty percent, most of this should not be an agent at all, and the honest design is a deterministic workflow with a language model at the edges: intent classification in, natural language out.
  • Identity propagation spike. A two-week proof that an end user's token survives the full chain and that a downstream call rejects a request the user is not entitled to make. This is the gate on the entire state tier and it should be attempted before anything else is built.
  • Capability server contract standard. What every application team must publish: capability ids, tiers, input and output schemas, idempotency semantics, dry-run support, error taxonomy, ownership and on-call. Model Context Protocol per domain is the natural fit today. Without a standard you will negotiate twelve bilateral integrations.
  • Evaluation design. Golden set per intent, offline regression gates on prompt, model, and index changes, online feedback capture, and a defined bar for what accuracy is good enough to keep serving. Decide who owns the golden set and how it grows from production misses.
  • Knowledge curation operating model. Four corpora, four owners, four decay rates. Who is accountable for freshness, what the review cadence is, how deprecated content is tombstoned rather than deleted, and how a document's provenance and last-verified date surface in an answer.
  • Failure-domain design. Where Pathfinder runs, what it depends on, and what it can still answer when the estate is degraded. Includes a cached-runbook read path that survives the loss of Splunk, Dynatrace, and ServiceNow simultaneously.
  • Action safety catalogue. For every write capability: reversibility, blast radius, approval requirement, dry-run behaviour, idempotency strategy, rate limit, and rollback procedure. Any capability without all seven does not ship.
  • Cost model per intent. Token cost, tool-call cost, and telemetry query cost per answered question, with a per-intent budget. Splunk and Dynatrace queries are not free and an agent that fans out on every turn will find that out in production.
  • Human factors at three in the morning. How does an engineer verify what Pathfinder told them, quickly, when they are tired and the clock is running? Citations, confidence expression, and a one-click path to the underlying evidence are load-bearing, not polish. A tool that is usually right and occasionally confidently wrong is more dangerous during an incident than no tool.
  • Audit evidence package. What you can produce when asked to explain a specific action six months later: decision log, policy version, retrieved context, model version, prompt version, approver identity, and the full trace. Design this before the first write capability, not after the first finding.
  • Adoption and trust curve. If the first three answers a team receives are wrong, that team will not come back, and no amount of later accuracy recovers them. Plan the rollout cohort by cohort with a deliberate accuracy bar per cohort rather than a general availability date.
